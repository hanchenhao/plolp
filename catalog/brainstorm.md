
# Brainstorm

Always invite LLM to think through your challenge from multiple perspectives. Discuss with it and consolidate your thoughts.

## Motivation

A straightforward prompt to an LLM typically yields a single answer or perspective. That answer might not be the best or the only solution. Especially for complex, creative, or open-ended tasks, focusing on just one approach can severely limit the quality of the outcome. Without a brainstorming process, you may miss out on alternative solutions or the chance to combine ideas into a better answer.

## Solution

Treat your interaction with the LLM as a brainstorming session rather than a one-shot Q&A. In practice, this means prompting the model to generate multiple ideas, options, or approaches before you commit to a final answer. For example, you might ask the model, “What are some different ways to approach this problem?” or “List a few possible solutions to consider.” By explicitly inviting a range of ideas, you encourage the LLM to think beyond the most obvious response.

Once you have a list of ideas from the model, the next step is to explore and refine them. Don’t settle immediately on the first suggestion. Instead, discuss the options with the LLM: ask for more detail on a promising idea, inquire about the pros and cons of each approach, or request comparisons between them. This iterative back-and-forth helps identify which ideas are most viable or innovative. Finally, you can ask the model to help consolidate the best aspects of the discussion into a coherent solution. This collaborative exploration between you and the LLM leads to a more creative, well-considered result than a single-pass answer ever could.

## Challenge

Have you ever asked an AI assistant a question and felt that the answer was a bit too plain or one-dimensional? This is a common experience when using LLMs in a straightforward, single-pass way. Many users treat the LLM as a one-shot answer machine: you pose your question, get a single response, and move on. While this approach can work for simple queries, it often falls short for more complex or creative challenges. The first answer the LLM gives might be *technically* correct or plausible, but it might not be the most insightful, innovative, or well-rounded solution. In fact, stopping at that first answer means you’re likely leaving better ideas unexplored.

### Why a Single Answer Isn’t Enough

Large language models are trained to give a reasonable answer based on the prompt, but they usually aim for a single, coherent response rather than a range of alternatives. When you ask a straightforward question, the model will try to produce what it thinks is the most appropriate answer. This single answer might reflect the most common solution found in its training data, or the most obvious interpretation of your question. As a result, the response can be generic or biased toward a particular perspective. For example, if you ask, “How should we handle this project issue?” the LLM might pick one strategy and elaborate on it. That one strategy could be useful, but what about other approaches you haven’t considered? The model *could* provide those too, but not unless you prompt it to explore further.

### Complex Problems Require Multiple Angles

Consider any complicated issue you face in your work. Rarely is there a single silver-bullet solution that addresses every facet. A policy challenge, for instance, might involve social, economic, and logistical factors. Tackling it from only one angle means other aspects get overlooked. The same goes for creative tasks like drafting a program proposal or designing a new service—the first idea that comes to mind might be acceptable, but it’s usually not the most innovative or comprehensive. By default, an LLM doesn’t know to give you that wider view. If your prompt doesn’t explicitly ask for variety, the model will likely stick to one train of thought. This can inadvertently narrow the outcome. You might end up with an answer that addresses part of the problem well but ignores other important perspectives.

### Missing Out on Alternative Solutions

When you stop at a single answer, you may be settling for a mediocre approach without realizing it. Think of a brainstorming session you might have with colleagues: the first suggestions are often conventional, but as more ideas flow, someone eventually proposes something unexpected and brilliant. LLMs are capable of the same range of thinking *if you prompt them to stretch out*. Without prompting, they’ll usually give a safe, average answer.

For example, a civil servant might ask an LLM, “How do I improve public transportation in our city?” and the model could respond with a standard solution like “increase bus routes.” That’s a valid idea, but what about improving the scheduling system, introducing subsidized fares, or encouraging ride-sharing? Those alternatives may never come up if the user doesn’t push for brainstorming. If the civil servant implemented only the first idea, they might later discover that the real bottleneck was not the number of buses but poor timing or affordability. A broader brainstorming session from the start would have highlighted such insights, potentially saving time and resources by guiding the plan in a better direction.

In many cases, the *best* answer isn’t one of the initial ideas outright; it’s a hybrid of several. If you only ever get one idea from the LLM, you’ll never discover those hybrids. Brainstorming exposes you to multiple pieces of the puzzle. You and the LLM can then pick the best parts of each and assemble a solution that’s more robust than any single idea alone. This synergy is hard to achieve if the conversation ends after one answer.

### Overcoming Your Own Blind Spots

Another challenge with single-pass prompting is that it often reinforces the asker’s existing assumptions. If you have a particular idea in mind (even subconsciously), the LLM’s first answer might align with that bias, especially if your question was framed narrowly. You might then accept that answer because it sounds “right” or familiar. However, what if your perspective is incomplete? By engaging in a brainstorming dialogue, you force both yourself and the model to venture beyond the initial viewpoint. The LLM might surface considerations you hadn’t thought of. In a way, you’re using the AI to challenge your own thinking. For instance, suppose you draft a policy memo and the first solution the model offers fits perfectly with your department’s usual approach. If you stop there, you miss the opportunity to bring in fresh ideas from outside that comfort zone. A brainstorming prompt like, “Can you suggest some unconventional approaches as well?” might reveal an angle that surprises you—in a good way.

### The Value of Iteration with the AI

Brainstorming with an LLM isn’t just about getting a list of ideas and stopping there. It’s an iterative process of idea generation and evaluation. This can feel unusual at first if you’re used to getting one quick answer. But think of the LLM as a collaborator that’s always available to delve deeper. Each follow-up question you ask can unveil more detail or a new perspective. Importantly, this iterative engagement is where average ideas are sifted out and good ideas are polished.

Don't worry that you’re “bothering” the AI by asking for more ideas or detail—it doesn’t mind. The model doesn’t get tired or impatient, and it thrives on more input. In fact, an LLM is often underutilized if you only tap it for one answer. By continuing the dialogue, you’re letting a brainstorming partner speak freely instead of cutting it off after the first suggestion. The conversation can go in directions you didn’t anticipate—perhaps revealing a hidden pitfall in one approach or an opportunity in another. Without this back-and-forth, you might remain unaware of those nuances.

Yes, the brainstorming approach means spending a few more turns of conversation. However, those extra minutes upfront can save you hours later. Refining ideas with the LLM early on prevents you from pursuing a dead-end solution or having to redo work because a better idea was left unexplored. In a professional setting, the improved quality of the final output—be it a policy draft, a project plan, or a report—more than justifies the small additional effort.

### Brainstorming as a Best Practice

Brainstorming is a time-tested method in problem-solving and creative work. When humans brainstorm, we deliberately generate a variety of ideas without immediately judging or filtering them. This practice often leads to breakthroughs after a series of initial, obvious suggestions are out of the way. Not every idea mentioned in a brainstorm will be usable—some will be too costly, too wild, or just not feasible—but that’s expected. The point is to get all possibilities on the table first. Using an LLM should be no different. Instead of viewing the AI as a source of one “correct” answer, you leverage it as a brainstorming partner that can toss out many ideas. Some ideas will be routine, some might be off-the-wall, but among them could be the gem that truly solves your problem. The Brainstorm pattern formalizes this practice in the context of LLMs: it reminds you that the first answer is just the starting point, not the finish line.

*(Behind the scenes, an LLM’s training makes it likely to offer the most statistically probable answer to a question as its default response. Often that “most probable” answer corresponds to common knowledge or a conventional approach. It takes additional prompting to get the model to venture into less obvious territory. Think of it as gently prodding the AI to not just give you the safe answer, but also the creative ones that are lurking a bit deeper.)*

**When to Use Brainstorming with LLMs:** Not every query needs a brainstorming session—if you need a quick factual answer, a single response might do. But whenever you face a task that is open-ended, multifaceted, or calls for creativity, it’s a good candidate for the Brainstorm pattern. Some examples include:  
- **Policy and Strategy Development:** When drafting policies or plans, ask the LLM for multiple policy options or program ideas before zeroing in. This ensures you explore a wide range of possible interventions.  
- **Report Writing and Analysis:** If you need to write a comprehensive report or analysis, brainstorm different points to cover or perspectives to include. The model can suggest aspects of the topic you might otherwise miss.  
- **Creative Writing and Communication:** For writing speeches, proposals, or press releases, have the LLM propose different themes or narrative approaches. One angle might turn out far more effective than the most obvious one.  
- **General Problem-Solving:** Anytime you’re tackling a problem that doesn’t have a single obvious solution (like improving a service, reducing costs, or increasing community engagement), prompt the AI to brainstorm various solutions. This gives you a menu of options to evaluate instead of a take-it-or-leave-it answer.

In short, the challenge that Brainstorm addresses is the tendency to settle for the first answer. By recognizing this limitation and deliberately engaging the LLM in a multi-turn ideation process, you drastically improve the breadth and quality of the outcomes. Instead of a narrow, one-shot response, you get a richer palette of ideas and the insight needed to choose the best path forward. The next section will show how this works in practice, guiding you through an example of the Brainstorm pattern in action.

## Example

To see the Brainstorm pattern in action, let’s walk through a hypothetical scenario. Imagine John, a policy advisor at the Ministry of Education, is tasked with reducing the high school dropout rate in his region. This is a complex challenge with many possible causes and solutions. John knows from experience that if he just asks the LLM for a solution in one go, he'll get a generic answer that barely scratches the surface. Instead of accepting a mediocre first draft, he decides to use the LLM as a thinking partner to explore the problem thoroughly.

John starts the session by explicitly inviting the LLM to brainstorm, treating it like a colleague in a planning meeting:

**John:** *What are some possible initiatives or approaches to help reduce high school dropout rates? Please give me several distinct ideas.*

**LLM:**  
- **Mentorship and Tutoring Programs:** Establish after-school mentoring or tutoring initiatives where at-risk students are paired with mentors or receive extra academic help. This support can address academic struggles that often lead to dropouts.  
- **Parental and Community Engagement:** Increase involvement of parents and the local community in students’ education. For instance, hold regular workshops or mentoring sessions where community leaders emphasize the value of staying in school. Strong support networks can motivate students to graduate.  
- **Alternative Education Pathways:** Develop flexible schooling options such as vocational training programs, evening classes, or online learning for students who struggle in a traditional school setting. Providing different paths to a diploma can keep students engaged who might otherwise drop out.

*John now has three distinct ideas on the table. He’s pleased to see that the model offered a variety of approaches, including angles he hadn’t initially considered. Notably, these ideas cover different aspects of the problem: academic support (mentoring/tutoring), social support (community engagement), and structural changes (alternative education paths). If he had asked a generic question and taken the first response, the LLM might have focused on just one of these areas—perhaps suggesting a tutoring program or another single solution. By explicitly requesting several distinct ideas, John ensured a broader view of the solution space from the very start.*

Having gathered a range of options, John’s next step is to understand them better. He wants to know the potential upsides and downsides of each approach before deciding how to proceed.

**John:** *Thanks! Now, can you help me compare these options? What are the pros and cons of each of these three approaches?*

**LLM:**  
**Mentorship and Tutoring Programs:**  
- *Pros:* Provides direct academic support, which can quickly improve grades and confidence. Often relatively low-cost since it can leverage volunteer mentors or existing staff. Targets students who need help the most.  
- *Cons:* May require consistent volunteer involvement or funding for tutors. It addresses academic issues but might not solve non-academic factors of dropping out (like family issues or lack of engagement).

**Parental and Community Engagement:**  
- *Pros:* Builds a support network around students. When students feel their community is invested in their success, they often feel more motivated. Can improve communication between school and home, addressing problems early.  
- *Cons:* Can be difficult to get busy parents and community members consistently involved. Results might take time and are harder to measure. Not all students have supportive family environments to begin with, which can limit impact.

**Alternative Education Pathways:**  
- *Pros:* Offers customized solutions for students who don’t thrive in traditional settings. Can re-engage students by aligning education with their interests or life circumstances (for example, giving a hands-on learner a vocational track). Helps prevent those who struggle academically or financially from feeling that dropping out is their only option.  
- *Cons:* Requires resources to develop and maintain multiple pathways (new curricula, training, etc.). Might face resistance from those who believe in a one-size-fits-all approach to education. Also, if not executed well, could inadvertently track some students into “lesser” programs.

*By reviewing the pros and cons, John gets a clearer picture of each option. The LLM’s analysis highlights that every idea has strengths and weaknesses. For instance, mentorship programs directly help academics but might not fix issues at home, whereas community engagement builds support but can be hard to sustain. Through this comparative breakdown, John can make a more informed decision about which approach (or which combination of approaches) might work best. This step illustrates the “discussion” phase of brainstorming—John is effectively asking the LLM to evaluate the brainstormed ideas critically, just as a team might debate options in a meeting.*

Now John thinks about how to act on this information. It strikes him that the first two ideas might complement each other. Why choose one when a combined strategy could harness the benefits of both? He decides to ask the LLM for a merged plan that incorporates **mentorship/tutoring** alongside **community engagement**. This way, he can tackle the issue from two angles at once.

**John:** *It looks like both the mentorship program and community engagement could work well together. Can you help me outline a plan that combines those two approaches?*

**LLM:** Combining "Mentorship and Tutoring" with "Community Engagement" could look like a comprehensive **Community Mentorship Initiative**. Here’s an outline of how such a combined plan might work:

1. **Recruit & Train Mentors:** Identify volunteers from the community (local college students, retired teachers, professionals) and train them to mentor and tutor high-risk students. Emphasize cultural or community connections between mentors and students to build trust.  
2. **Student Matching:** Pair each participating student with a dedicated mentor who meets with them weekly for tutoring help and coaching. The mentor not only addresses academic needs but also acts as a positive role model.  
3. **Community Workshops & Events:** Organize monthly school events involving parents, mentors, and community leaders. These workshops might cover study skills, career talks by community members, or parent–teacher discussions. This increases parent involvement and shows students that the whole community cares about their success.  
4. **Support & Monitoring:** Have school counselors or program coordinators check in on each mentor–student pair. Provide resources (like tutoring materials or counseling services) as needed. Track attendance and grades for early signs of trouble, and engage the mentor and family if issues arise.  
5. **Recognition & Incentives:** Celebrate improvements and successes publicly. For example, hold an annual “Student Success Night” to recognize students who improved their performance or mentors who made a big impact. This keeps the community engaged and rewards students for staying in school.

This combined approach leverages the direct academic support of mentorship while also building a support network around the student. The mentoring addresses immediate academic issues and personal guidance, and the community engagement component fosters an environment that values education and keeps students motivated.

**John:** *We also had the idea of alternative education pathways. Do you think we should include something about that in this plan, or perhaps tackle it separately?*

**LLM:** Alternative pathways (like vocational training or flexible classes) could certainly complement the mentorship and community efforts, but it might be a lot to implement all at once. One approach could be to pilot the mentorship/community program first and, if successful, then expand to introduce an alternative pathway for students who are still struggling. Alternatively, the plan could include a partnership with an existing vocational program in the community, so mentors and counselors have that option to suggest to students when appropriate. It really depends on resources and how much change the school system can manage at one time. If the goal is to start with a focused effort, keeping the current plan as-is might be best, with a note that alternative schooling options are a future consideration.

*John briefly considered including the third idea (alternative pathways) as well, but with the LLM’s advice he decides it might be best as a future expansion once the mentorship/community program is underway. This shows that brainstorming can surface more ideas than you ultimately use – and that’s okay. The key is that John made an informed choice, rather than never thinking of that option at all.

At the end of the session, John has a well-rounded program he can confidently propose. By brainstorming with the LLM, he combined two complementary ideas into a single plan and duly noted a third idea for later. The result tackles the problem from multiple angles: direct help for students and a community push to keep them engaged, with an eye on alternative education for the future. Had he simply asked for a dropout reduction plan in one go, he likely would have received something much more generic – perhaps focused only on tutoring or on parent outreach in isolation. Instead, through iterative prompting, John arrived at a nuanced solution that is far more comprehensive.

This example demonstrates how the Brainstorm pattern leads to better outcomes. John didn’t stop at the first idea or the first draft. He used the LLM to explore alternatives, examine them critically, and then refine them into a stronger final product. You can apply the same approach to your own challenges: generate ideas, discuss them with the LLM, and synthesize the best parts. The process might involve a few more back-and-forth steps, but as we see, it unlocks creative solutions that a single-pass answer would never have produced.*

**Key Takeaways from the Brainstorm Example:**
- **Start Broad:** John began by asking for several ideas, which ensured a diverse set of solutions (brainstorming divergent ideas).
- **Examine Options:** He then asked the LLM to analyze those ideas, revealing the strengths and weaknesses of each.
- **Refine and Synthesize:** Rather than picking one idea as-is, John combined the best aspects of two approaches and had the LLM flesh out a detailed plan (converging on a solution).
- **Result:** The final plan was more comprehensive and robust than any single idea alone. It addressed multiple facets of the problem and was something John might not have conceived without an iterative dialogue.

Applying the Brainstorm pattern effectively turns the LLM from a mere question-answering tool into a collaborative partner for problem-solving and creativity.
