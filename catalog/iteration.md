# Iteration  
*Learn from mistakes and improve intentionally; avoid aimless experimenting.*

## Motivation

Making random changes to your prompt without a strategy can waste time and not lead to better results. Likewise, if you don't iterate at all, any weaknesses in your initial prompt remain unaddressed. Without intentional iteration — analyzing the output and refining your prompt based on it — you might either stick with a flawed prompt or wander in circles with changes that don't actually help.

## Solution

Refine your prompts through deliberate iteration. After each response the LLM gives, assess what was good and what fell short. Then adjust your next prompt to specifically fix those issues or to test a hypothesis about improving the answer. This might involve clarifying an instruction, adding a detail, or reordering parts of the prompt. By systematically learning from each attempt (rather than changing things at random), you guide the prompt toward an increasingly effective form and avoid unnecessary trial-and-error.

## Challenge

Even with the right mindset of continuous improvement (as we saw in the **Never Stop Winning** pattern), many users struggle with **how** to iterate effectively. There are two common pitfalls at play. On one hand, some users make changes haphazardly, hoping that something will stick. On the other, some users avoid making any changes at all, sticking with a flawed prompt out of habit or uncertainty. Both approaches hinder progress. Let’s look at these challenges in more detail:

1. **Aimless Tinkering (Random Edits):** This is the trap of trying lots of adjustments with no clear plan. Imagine a user who, after receiving a mediocre LLM output, starts tweaking their prompt arbitrarily: adding a sentence here, removing a word there, throwing in fancy terms or extra instructions just based on a gut feeling. In each round, they hit "Enter" again hoping for a magic improvement. Often the result is confusion — maybe one version seems slightly better in one aspect and worse in another, but the user isn't sure why. Without a method, this process becomes like wandering in the dark. The user isn’t learning from each attempt because they change too many things at once or at random. One moment they ask for a "*detailed summary*", the next they switch to "*make it concise*", then they include an example, then they remove it — all without pausing to analyze the effect of each change. This aimless tinkering not only wastes time but can also lead the user in circles. The frustration builds as they wonder, *“Why am I not getting a better answer? I’ve tried everything!”* In reality, they haven’t tried a systematic approach — they’ve just been throwing darts blindly. The challenge here is lack of intentionality: without a clear goal for each iteration, it's hard to know whether a change helped or not.

2. **Sticking with the First Draft (No Iteration):** The opposite problem is doing nothing at all beyond the initial attempt. Some users will run one prompt, get an output that is okay but not great, and then simply accept it as the best they can do. They might sense that the answer could be improved, but they don't know *how* to improve it, or they worry that changing the prompt might make things worse. So they end up settling for the status quo. For example, a civil servant might ask an LLM to "write a brief on policy X" and receive a response that has some relevant points but also some vague generalities or missing specifics. If they stick with that first draft, all the prompt's weaknesses remain in the final result. Perhaps the prompt forgot to specify the audience or the desired tone, and so the output is too generic or too technical — but since they didn't iterate, those issues persist. The user misses the opportunity to refine and tailor the output to their needs. Often the reason behind this stagnation is uncertainty about what to change or a misconception that "the AI gave me this, so that's all it can do." In other cases, it's simply the fear of venturing beyond the initial success, however modest. The challenge here is overcoming inertia. If you never iterate, you'll never discover how much better the result could have been with a few targeted tweaks.

Both of these pitfalls share a common issue: a lack of **intentional, goal-driven refinement**. Whether you’re paralyzed by uncertainty or flailing with random tweaks, the end result is the same — you don’t meaningfully improve the output. The key to breaking out of this is to approach each prompt attempt as a learning experiment. Instead of blindly guessing or giving up too soon, you treat every iteration as a chance to gain insight. The goal is not just "try again" but "try again *with a purpose*." Each time you run the model, you should have a hypothesis or specific aim in mind: perhaps "*If I provide more context, the summary will include the missing details I need,*" or "*Maybe reordering the instructions will make the output more structured.*" This way, you're not just hoping for luck — you're conducting a mini-experiment each time.

Another challenge is that users often don’t track what they’ve tried. When you make a dozen changes in a row without noting them, it's easy to lose track of what worked and what didn't. You might inadvertently reintroduce an issue you already solved, or you might discard a good idea because you couldn't remember which version made the improvement. Keeping a history of your prompt iterations is crucial. This is where the **Prompt File pattern** (discussed earlier in this book) becomes incredibly useful. By writing your prompt in a document and saving versions (Prompt_v1, Prompt_v2, etc.), you create a paper trail of your thought process. You can refer back to earlier prompts to see how far you’ve come and which changes led to which outcomes. Without this, it's like trying to improve a recipe without writing down any of the ingredients you added — you might end up making the same mistake twice or failing to repeat something that actually worked well. Not using a prompt file or other logging method means you risk **losing your wins**; any insight gained in one iteration could be forgotten in the next.

Finally, there's the temptation to try to fix everything in one go. Seeing a bunch of problems in an output, some users will attempt a massive overhaul of the prompt to address all issues at once — changing the task description, adding a ton of new info, tweaking the format, all simultaneously. This "big bang" approach is risky. If the next output comes out better, you won't be sure which of the many changes actually helped. If it comes out worse, you're left guessing which change caused the regression. A more sustainable approach is incremental improvement: tackle one significant change at a time or a small handful of related changes. Think of it like editing a draft document (or adjusting a recipe): if you alter twenty things between test runs and something breaks (or fixes), diagnosing the cause is hard. But if you change one thing and test, you have a clear read on its impact. Similarly, in prompt iteration, making smaller adjustments lets you isolate the effect of each tweak. It might feel slower in the moment, but it's far more efficient in getting you to a high-quality result with understanding intact. Remember, the aim of iteration isn't just to change the output, but to deepen your understanding of how the LLM responds. Each iteration should teach you something, even if the output didn't dramatically improve. Over time, these lessons stack up, and you become much better at prompting.

## Example

To see how intentional iteration works in practice, let’s walk through a scenario. Imagine a user named Bob, who is drafting an important announcement email using an LLM. Bob works at a city education department and needs to announce a new policy to local school principals. He’s already taken the lesson from **Never Stop Winning** to heart: he's willing to try multiple attempts. Now he’s focusing on *how* to iterate effectively on his prompt to get the best email draft.

Bob starts with a straightforward prompt in his prompt file:

*"Draft an email announcing the new 'Healthy Lunch Initiative' policy to all school principals. Explain the main points of the policy."*

He then runs this prompt (let’s call this *Attempt #1*). The LLM produces a draft email. Bob reads it carefully. The draft is polite and gets the general idea across, but it's **very generic**. It says things like *"We are implementing a new policy to improve student health"* without mentioning specific details such as what exactly the policy entails (for example, does it change the cafeteria menu? Does it ban certain foods? Are there new guidelines for principals?). It also doesn't mention any next steps or what he expects the principals to do. Bob notices these shortcomings and writes them down on a notepad: "Missing specifics of policy (what changes?), no call to action for principals, tone a bit too generic."

Writing down these observations is a crucial step. It helps Bob set clear goals for the next iteration. Instead of just thinking "make it better," he has a short list of concrete improvements to tackle one by one. On a whiteboard by his desk (or a sticky note on his monitor), Bob jots down a few key points to address: **Include policy details**, **Add call-to-action**, **Refine tone (more direct)**. These will guide his next moves.

For *Attempt #2*, Bob edits his prompt rather than starting from scratch. Recalling the **Attachment pattern** from earlier, he decides to provide the LLM with more context. He finds a section from the policy document that lists the main provisions (e.g., "schools must offer at least two vegetarian options daily; sugary drinks will be phased out," etc.) and includes that as an attachment or pasted text in the prompt file. He also appends a line to his prompt: *"Include the specific provisions of the Healthy Lunch Initiative in the email."* Additionally, to ensure there's a call-to-action, he adds: *"End the email by advising principals on the next steps they should take (for example, updating lunch menus or scheduling a staff meeting to discuss implementation)."* Bob saves this as Prompt_v2 in his prompt file and runs it.

The result of Attempt #2 is significantly improved in some areas. The new draft email now clearly **lists the key policy details**, which makes it far more informative. It also concludes with a paragraph that instructs principals to, for example, "review your current lunch offerings and plan a meeting with your cafeteria staff to align with the new guidelines." This addresses two of Bob's main goals: covering the missing specifics and adding a call-to-action. Bob feels the content is much closer to what he needs. However, upon reviewing the email, he notices a new issue: the draft has become a bit **long-winded and formal**. The policy details are there, but the tone has veered into very bureaucratic language, and the email is perhaps too lengthy for busy principals to read through. Bob suspects this happened because he dumped a whole chunk of policy text in, and the LLM tried to be very thorough with it.

Now, Bob faces a decision point common in iteration: something improved (content) but something else got weaker (tone/length). Here, Bob practices another important habit of iteration — evaluating which changes to keep and which to adjust further. He definitely wants to keep the detailed content (that was a win for Attempt #2). The tone, however, needs refinement. This is a good outcome because it gives Bob a clear focus for *Attempt #3*: **improve the clarity and tone** without losing the detail.

For *Attempt #3*, Bob decides not to remove the policy details (since those are essential), but to adjust how the prompt guides the tone and conciseness. He adds a line in the prompt file like: *"Use a clear, friendly tone, and keep the email concise (no more than 3 short paragraphs)."* He hopes this will push the LLM to write in a more approachable style and trim any unnecessary fluff. He also reflects on the formal language issue and realizes he hadn't specified the tone at all in the previous attempts, so the LLM defaulted to a rather stiff style. By explicitly requesting a friendlier tone now, he aims to fix that. Bob saves these changes as Prompt_v3 and runs it.

Attempt #3 yields an email that is concise, down-to-earth, and still informative. The draft now opens with a warm greeting, briefly states the policy's purpose, bullet-points a few key provisions of the Healthy Lunch Initiative, and ends with a clear call-to-action for the principals. The tone is professional but not overly formal — it sounds like one colleague speaking to others, rather than an impersonal memo. Importantly, it's shorter and more likely to be read fully. Bob compares the Attempt #3 output with Attempt #2 and is pleased to see improvement in tone and length while preserving the important details. His iterative changes achieved what he wanted.

But iteration doesn’t always go so smoothly in a straight line. Let’s imagine that instead, Attempt #3 had an unexpected side effect: suppose the instruction to be concise caused the LLM to omit one of the key policy provisions from the email, sacrificing a bit of completeness. If that happened, Bob would need to decide how to address it. He has a few options, guided by his iterative mindset: he could *rollback* that last change and try a different approach (for example, maybe the "no more than 3 paragraphs" constraint was too tight and he could remove or loosen that while still asking for a friendly tone). Or he might keep the tone instruction and instead tweak how the policy details are requested (maybe explicitly tell the model, "include **all** the listed provisions in the email"). The important part is that Bob is willing to reverse a decision if it doesn't work out. Rolling back a change isn't a failure; it's part of the process. In software development, teams often revert a code change that introduced a bug — here, Bob would be doing the same with his prompt. By comparing the outputs and seeing that one element got worse, he can target that in the next iteration. In this hypothetical case, maybe *Attempt #4* would combine the successes of #2 and #3: it keeps the friendly, concise tone *and* explicitly ensures all key details are included.

After a few careful iterations, Bob finalizes a prompt that consistently produces a great email draft whenever he runs it. Throughout this process, each iteration taught him something: he learned that providing actual data (policy details) drastically improves specificity; he learned that tone and style need explicit guidance; he learned that brevity can be requested and adjusted. Moreover, because he saved each version of the prompt, he can always look back and recall how he arrived at the final prompt. If a colleague asks for help or if he tackles a similar task in the future, he has those intermediate versions to illustrate the journey or reuse pieces of them.

**Key techniques from Bob's example:** Bob’s story highlights several techniques of effective iteration. First, he analyzed the output of each attempt to pinpoint specific shortcomings (generic content, missing details, wrong tone) instead of vaguely saying "this isn't good." Second, he made targeted changes to address those points (adding context, specifying tone, etc.) rather than random edits. Third, he changed one major aspect at a time, which made it easier to identify what worked. Fourth, he kept a record of each prompt version and used simple tools like notes and checklists to stay focused on one improvement at a time. Finally, he wasn't afraid to undo a change that had unintended negative effects. All of these habits made his iteration process productive rather than frustrating.

Through intentional iteration, you gradually hone in on an excellent prompt. Each cycle is a learning loop: you plan a tweak, you try it, you observe the result, and you reflect on the next tweak. Over time, this approach turns prompting into a skill that improves with experience. By iterating thoughtfully, you not only get a better answer from the LLM for your immediate task, but you also gain a deeper understanding of how to work with the model. In other words, every iteration makes *you* better at prompting. Even if a particular attempt doesn’t hit the jackpot, it’s never truly a failure — it’s a step forward in mastery.
