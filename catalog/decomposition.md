# Decomposition  
*Break tasks into smaller, non-overlapping subtasks.*

## Motivation  
A complex request presented as one large task can easily overwhelm a large language model (LLM) and lead to a disorganized or incomplete answer. When you ask the model to do everything at once, it might overlook some parts of the task or jumble different issues together. In a single giant prompt, important details can get lost and the response may lack clarity or depth. Without breaking the problem apart, the LLM struggles to address all aspects thoroughly, and the final output often misses details or feels scattershot.

## Solution  
The remedy is to split the problem into clear, manageable subtasks and tackle them one by one. Make sure each subtask is distinct – no overlapping responsibilities – and that together the subtasks cover every part of the original challenge. In other words, nothing important is left out, but nothing is addressed twice. You can then guide the LLM through each smaller task in sequence, or ask it to outline the steps and solve them stepwise. This approach follows the old consulting principle of making components *“mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.”* In plain terms, each piece of the problem is separate and unique, and all the pieces together solve the whole problem. By doing this, you help the model focus on one aspect at a time. The result is a more structured, complete answer, since the LLM can dedicate its full attention to each component of the problem in turn.

## Challenge  
Even when you understand the value of breaking a problem down, actually doing it poses its own challenges. Many users, especially those new to working with large language models, struggle with how to **think about** a complex task in the first place. It’s tempting to throw a big question at the AI and hope it magically covers everything. After all, it *sounds* efficient – one prompt, one answer. But in practice, this “all-in-one” approach often leaves you with an output that’s hard to untangle or incomplete. The mental habit of decomposition doesn’t always come naturally, and that’s where many people hit a wall.

### Facing Complexity Overload  
One common challenge is sheer **overwhelm**. Imagine you have a multifaceted project, like preparing a detailed business report that includes market research, financial analysis, and strategy recommendations. If you just tell the LLM, “Write me a full business report with all these sections,” you’re likely to get a response that’s uneven. The model might nail one part (say, the market research) but gloss over another (perhaps the financials) because it’s juggling everything at once. As the user, you then face a daunting output that mixes multiple topics without a clear structure. It feels a bit like asking someone to draft an entire book in one sitting — the quality will suffer because it’s too much to handle at once. The **complexity overload** can result in confusion not only for the model but for you as you try to parse the answer.

This overwhelm happens because large tasks often contain smaller hidden tasks. When you present everything in one big question, the LLM doesn’t inherently know how to prioritize or organize the response. It has to guess what structure or breakdown you might have intended. Sometimes it guesses wrong. 

For instance, it might spend most of its answer on the first part of your question and give cursory treatment to the rest. Users new to LLMs might interpret this as the AI being “not good enough” at the request, when in reality the issue is how the task was presented. The challenge is recognizing that **the way you frame the problem** directly affects the outcome.

### The Temptation of the One-Shot Prompt  
Another obstacle is the natural temptation to use a **one-shot prompt** for everything. It feels direct: you have a big problem, so you ask a big question. For example, a user might ask, “Help me plan a conference event, including venue selection, speaker lineup, catering, marketing, and budget.” This prompt is comprehensive, but that’s exactly the problem – it’s too comprehensive for a single step. The LLM will attempt to produce a plan covering all those aspects in one go. What you often get back is a mixed bag: a few bullet points about each area, probably lacking depth or missing some considerations. The user in this scenario might find the answer disappointingly shallow: “It mentioned social media marketing but nothing about traditional marketing, and the budget section was just a generic paragraph.”

Why do we fall for this one-shot approach? Partly because it’s how we’re used to interacting with computers – you ask a question, you get an answer. Also, there’s a bit of wishful thinking involved: if the AI is so smart, shouldn’t it figure out the whole complex task on its own? In reality, even a very intelligent model benefits from guidance. 

Think of it like giving instructions to a team member. If you had an assistant at work, you wouldn’t just say, “Handle the entire conference planning,” and leave it at that. You’d break the job into parts, maybe asking them first to research venues, then later to draft a marketing plan, and so on. Yet with AI, users often skip this management step. The challenge is shifting your mindset to treat the AI more like a team member who needs a project plan, rather than a miracle worker that can do it all in one shot.

### Overlooking Parts of the Problem  
When we don’t consciously break down a problem, there’s a real risk of **overlooking important parts**. A complex task usually has multiple dimensions, but if you’re thinking about it in a lump, some dimensions might not even come to mind until after you see the output (and notice what’s missing). For instance, say you ask an LLM to “write a detailed report on improving employee satisfaction in our company.” That sounds straightforward, but “employee satisfaction” is broad. If you don’t specify subtasks, the AI might focus heavily on, say, workplace culture and forget to cover compensation or career development. Only after reading the answer do you realize, *oh, I should have also asked about training programs or office ergonomics*. The challenge here is that humans might not list out every facet of a big issue unless they deliberately take a moment to break it down.

This is where the **collectively exhaustive** part of the solution principle comes in as a thinking challenge. It requires you to pause and consider: what are *all* the pieces of this puzzle? For someone not used to systematic problem-solving, that pause might not happen.

They dive in and trust the AI to be exhaustive, which it rarely is without guidance. There’s a bit of mental discipline involved in stepping back and enumerating the components of the task. It might feel tedious, but it’s essential.

Many of us have not been formally trained in structured thinking – it’s a skill often taught in consulting or engineering, but less so in other fields. Consultants, for example, use formal frameworks to break down problems (the MECE principle we mentioned is one such tool popularized by McKinsey). In fact, books like *The McKinsey Way* offer insight into this structured approach to thinking. But even if you haven’t read that, you can practice the same idea with your AI prompts.

So, a user might not naturally think, *let me list the key areas of this problem*. Instead, they hold a fuzzy mental model of the task and hope the AI will fill in the blanks. The reality is, if you don’t bring up a topic in your prompt, there’s a good chance the model won’t cover it either.

### The Effort of Planning (and Why It’s Worth It)  
Let’s face it: breaking a complex task into parts takes a bit of effort and planning. This is another challenge – **effort avoidance**. Crafting multiple prompts or a step-by-step plan sounds like more work than simply asking your question once. Especially for non-technical professionals who just want a quick answer, the idea of playing project manager to an AI can be off-putting. You might think, “Do I really need to do all this? Can’t the AI just figure it out?” It’s understandable; we often look for the path of least resistance. Planning out subtasks can feel like a chore, and if you’re not accustomed to it, you might not know where to begin.

However, just as with any project, a little planning upfront can save a lot of time later. It’s similar to writing an outline before you draft a report or sorting your ingredients before you start cooking a complicated recipe. Without that preparation, you risk mid-task surprises and backtracking.

Many users learn the hard way: they try the quick one-prompt solution, get a mediocre result, and end up spending more time editing or re-asking the question. The challenge, therefore, is convincing yourself that a structured approach is worth the initial extra minutes. It can help to remember some familiar wisdom: “measure twice, cut once,” or “an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure.” In the context of LLMs, think of decomposition as that up-front investment that pays off with a cleaner, more useful answer with less need for revision.

There’s also the aspect of **skill development**. The first few times you break a problem into parts, it might feel slow or awkward. You have to consciously think about what the pieces should be. But with practice, this becomes second nature. Just as a seasoned event planner quickly jots down a checklist of tasks when asked to plan an event, a practiced LLM user will start automatically parsing a complex query into sub-queries. The challenge is sticking with it long enough for it to become a habit. Early on, you might need to write out the components or talk them through. Over time, you’ll find you can do it mentally on the fly. 

### Maintaining the Big Picture  
When you decompose a problem, another mental challenge is keeping track of the **big picture**. It’s possible to get so engrossed in the subtasks that you lose sight of the original goal. For example, if you break down that conference planning prompt into five separate prompts (venues, speakers, catering, marketing, budget), you might get five great answers for each subtopic. But now you have to synthesize those into a coherent plan. Some users find this stage tricky – it’s like having pieces of a puzzle and still needing to see how they all fit together. The integration step requires its own kind of thinking (we’ll address that in the Example section), but from a mental standpoint, you have to remember that each subtask is a means to an end, not the end itself.

This means as you work through parts, you should periodically zoom out and ask, “Am I still on track solving the overall problem?” If one subtask’s result seems off-track, you might need to adjust it or clarify it with another prompt. Some people worry that by focusing on pieces, they’ll end up with a disjointed outcome – five mini-answers that don’t gel. That’s a valid concern and highlights why **structure** is important in your breakdown. 

If your subtasks were truly mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, then integration is easier because together they naturally form the whole. But if you accidentally left a gap or created overlap, you may notice it when you try to combine results. This is normal; decomposition is a human thinking process and sometimes you won’t get it perfect on the first try. The key is to iterate: if you spot a gap, you can always ask another targeted prompt to fill it. If two subtasks gave overlapping information, you might need to decide which to use or how to reconcile them. Keeping the big picture in mind helps you catch these issues early.

### Shifting Your Mindset  
Ultimately, the biggest challenge in practicing decomposition is **shifting your mindset** about how to use AI as a tool. Many of us grew up with search engines where you type a full question or a few keywords and get a direct answer or a list of answers. Working with an LLM on a complex task is a different game. It’s more like collaborating with an intelligent partner. You wouldn’t expect a partner to do a multi-part job with zero guidance or discussion, and the same goes for an AI partner. The mental shift is in seeing a complex query not as a single question but as a project that you and the AI are tackling together.

This requires a bit of patience and a willingness to guide the AI through the task. It’s a collaborative mindset. Instead of “I ask, it answers,” think “I lead, it helps.” Some non-technical professionals find this empowering once they get used to it: you actually stay in control of the process, and the AI’s role becomes clearer and more effective. But getting to that point means unlearning the habit of treating the AI like a search box or a magic 8-ball that just spits out wisdom. You have to approach it with the same thoughtfulness you’d use when delegating work to a person.

In summary, the challenges of decomposition are mostly about our own thinking. We’re not training the AI here; we’re training ourselves to approach complex tasks methodically. Overcoming the temptation of the one-shot prompt, taking the time to map out the pieces of a problem, and maintaining an awareness of the whole as you handle parts — these are all hurdles in the mind. Recognizing them is the first step. Once you do, you’re prepared to apply decomposition techniques effectively, which leads to better and more reliable results from your AI partner.

## Example  
Now let’s put the concept of Decomposition into practice with concrete examples. There are several practical techniques for breaking down tasks, and which one you use can depend on the nature of your project. Here, we’ll explore four techniques: **Pipeline**, **Silo**, **Zoom In**, and **Divide and Integrate**. Each technique takes a slightly different angle on how to slice up a problem. We’ll illustrate each with a scenario that a non-technical professional might encounter, such as preparing a report or creating a newsletter. These examples are hypothetical, meant to show you how the approach works in real life.

### Pipeline: Stage-by-Stage Processing  
Think of the Pipeline technique as an assembly line for your task. You split a complex job into stages where the output of one stage becomes the input for the next. This is useful when the task has a natural sequence. For instance, imagine you need to **compose a detailed report on industry trends** for your team. This report requires gathering information from recent articles and then analyzing that information to write insights. Doing it in one go would be overwhelming for both you and the AI. Instead, you create a pipeline of prompts.

**Stage 1: Gathering Raw Material.** In the first stage, you might prompt the LLM with something like, “List the most significant industry news and trends from the past month in the tech sector, with brief summaries.” Here, you’re not yet asking for the final report, just the raw data or key points. The LLM will produce a list of recent developments: perhaps changes in market leaders, emerging technologies, regulatory updates, etc., each with a sentence or two of context.

This stage is all about breadth and collection. You’re ensuring that you have all the pieces you might need. It’s similar to doing research before writing an analysis. By the end of this stage, you have a compilation of facts and events – basically, the ingredients for your report.

**Stage 2: Analyzing and Writing the Report.** Now you take that output (the list of trends and summaries) and feed it into a second prompt. For example, you might say, “Using the above list of recent tech industry trends, write a 5-page analysis report explaining how these trends affect our business strategy.” Because you’ve done the preparation, the AI can focus on analysis and writing, rather than on recall or search. It will use the provided material to draw conclusions, connect dots, and produce a structured report.

The difference in quality is noticeable. If you had asked for the analysis report in one step, the model might have missed some trends or given very generic observations. Through the pipeline approach, the model first gathered specific data, and then it used that data to craft a more insightful and tailored report. You effectively guided it to first “read up” on the topic and then “write” about it, just as you would do yourself.

This Pipeline pattern can apply to many situations: first brainstorm ideas, then refine them; first collect requirements, then draft a proposal; first gather quotes and facts, then write an article. The key is identifying the stages inherent in the task and tackling them in order. By moving stage by stage, you reduce cognitive load on the model (and on yourself) and ensure no stage is rushed or skipped.

### Silo: Divide by Domain or Topic  
The Silo technique involves splitting a task by thematic areas or domains, each handled separately, and then bringing them together. This is handy when your overall task spans distinct subjects that require different treatments or expertise. Consider a scenario where you want to **create a weekly newsletter** for your organization. A typical newsletter might cover company news, industry news, and perhaps a spotlight on a team or project. Instead of prompting the AI for the entire newsletter in one go, you treat each section as its own mini-task – its own silo.

**Silo 1: Company Updates.** You might have one prompt dedicated to internal news: “Summarize the key company updates this week (e.g., new hires, project milestones, internal announcements) in a friendly tone.” The model focuses only on this domain and produces a concise update section. Because the prompt is narrowly scoped, the content is likely more thorough and specific – it’s not wandering off into industry news or other areas.

**Silo 2: Industry News.** Next, you create a separate prompt for industry-related content: “Provide a brief overview of important industry news this week relevant to our field, suitable for our newsletter.” This is analogous to the “geopolitics” vs “culture” example – here the domains are internal news vs external news. The LLM will generate a summary of relevant happenings in the broader industry, like competitor moves or market trends, without mixing in the company-specific info, because you isolated the context. This separate handling ensures clarity and avoids cross-talk between sections. If some information needs to appear in multiple sections, you can prepare it once and supply it to each relevant prompt (this reuse of content is discussed as the Attachment pattern in this book) instead of relying on the AI to generate it twice.

**Silo 3: Team Spotlight.** A third prompt could handle a spotlight section: “Draft a short feature highlighting the work of one of our teams or an employee achievement, including quotes and impact.” Again, this is separate from the others, focusing the AI on just one story or interview-style piece.

Once you have these siloed outputs, you then integrate them into one newsletter document. Since each piece was created in isolation, you may do a final pass (either manually or with a concluding AI prompt) to ensure the tone and style are consistent across sections. For example, you could prompt: “Combine the above sections into one cohesive newsletter, using a consistent friendly tone and adding transitions where necessary.” The AI will then stitch them together into a single narrative flow.

By splitting by topic, you took advantage of specialization. Each prompt was like an expert in its own niche: one “knew” all about the company internals, another focused on industry happenings, and another told a human story. This approach prevents one section from overshadowing or diluting another. If you tried to get the AI to cover all these disparate topics in one response, you might have ended up with a lopsided newsletter (too much on one topic, not enough on another) or generic statements. The Silo method ensures depth in each area before breadth across them.

### Zoom In: From Outline to Detail  
Zoom In is a technique where you start with a high-level overview or structure and then progressively enhance the detail. It’s like adjusting the zoom on a camera: first you see the whole scene, then you focus on specific parts to capture detail. This is especially useful for writing tasks where quality and coherence matter, such as **writing a book chapter or an in-depth article**. Instead of asking the AI to produce the polished chapter in one go, you guide it through stages of refinement.

**Step 1: High-Level Outline.** Begin by having the LLM generate a structured outline or a brief synopsis. For example: “Generate a detailed outline for a chapter about the benefits of remote work, including the main sections and bullet points for key arguments and examples.” The output should be an organized list of headings and sub-points: maybe an introduction, sections like “Productivity Impacts,” “Work-Life Balance,” “Challenges and Solutions,” and a conclusion. Under each section, there might be bullet points of what to cover (e.g., under productivity: fewer interruptions, flexible scheduling, etc.).

At this stage, you’re ensuring the chapter has a solid skeleton. It’s much easier to critique and adjust an outline than a full chapter. Maybe you see the outline and realize a section is missing, or the flow of topics needs rearranging – you can fix that now by tweaking the prompt or editing the outline yourself, then confirm with the AI if needed.

**Step 2: First Draft Expansion.** With a clear outline in hand, you then ask the model to write the chapter based on that structure. For instance: “Now write the full chapter based on the above outline, elaborating each point with explanations, examples, and a consistent, engaging tone.” The AI will produce a draft that follows the outline closely. Because you provided the map, the writing stays on track. Each section will correspond to a part of the outline, covering all the points you identified.

What you get is a comprehensive draft that hits all the planned topics. The quality at this point is typically much better than if you had attempted to write the chapter in one prompt from scratch. That’s because the model had the guidance of the outline – it knew where it was going next, preventing it from wandering off-topic or forgetting to address something.

**Step 3: Refine and Add Detail (if needed).** Depending on how the first draft looks, you might do another round of zooming in. Perhaps you notice some sections are a bit thin or some arguments could use evidence. Here you can prompt for enhancements: “Provide more detail and a real-world example for the section on Work-Life Balance benefits.” You could also use attachments or additional information at this stage to enrich the content (as hinted by the use of references or data). Essentially, you focus in on any weak spots and strengthen them, either through follow-up prompts or by supplying the AI with more context to incorporate.

After these steps, you might have a second draft that’s quite polished. You can ask the AI to proofread or you might do a final read-through yourself. The Zoom In approach has taken you from a bird’s-eye view to a fully fleshed-out piece methodically. It reduces the chance of missing the forest for the trees or vice versa. Each layer of zoom ensures the structure is solid and the details are filled in appropriately.

### Divide and Integrate: Conquer and Combine  
Divide and Integrate is the strategy to use when a task is naturally split into large independent chunks that eventually need to form a single whole. If Pipeline is about stages and Silo is about parallel topics, Divide and Integrate is about **big sections** that could almost stand alone, but you want them to come together coherently at the end. A classic scenario for this is **writing an entire multi-chapter report or book** using an LLM as an assistant. It’s an extension of Zoom In, but on a larger scale and with an explicit final integration step.

**Divide the Work:** Let’s say you are creating a comprehensive report or guide, consisting of several chapters or sections – for example, a guide on digital transformation with chapters on technology, people, and process. Rather than tackling it chapter by chapter in one sequence (which would be more like a pipeline of chapters), you might actually work on the major parts separately (maybe even simultaneously if you have a team or multiple AI instances). You could have one prompt or session devoted to drafting the “Technology” chapter, another for the “People” chapter, and another for the “Process” chapter. Each is a substantial piece in its own right, and by working on them independently, you allow each to develop fully without interference from the others. You might use Zoom In within each chunk (outline then draft), as we described above.

This divide approach acknowledges that sometimes different parts of a project don’t heavily depend on each other in the drafting phase. In our example, the technology recommendations might not affect what goes into the people section. So you have the freedom to generate those in parallel or just separately in time.

**Integrate the Results:** Once you have these separate pieces, you move to the integrate phase. Now the task is to weave them into a single, unified document. Integration can be its own prompt, such as: “Combine the three chapter drafts (Technology, People, Process) into one cohesive report. Ensure there’s a consistent tone and that references to earlier sections or later sections are aligned. Write an introduction that outlines the whole guide and a conclusion that ties everything together.”

The AI will take the already-written chapters and merge them. It will likely add transitions like referencing how a technology point affects people, or summarizing at the end how all these aspects interrelate. It might also harmonize language and style. If the chapters were written in isolation, maybe one had a more formal tone and another a more casual tone; the integration step can smooth that out.

As part of integration, you often create the glue content: an opening that introduces all the pieces and a closing that synthesizes them. In the book example, after writing all chapters, you might prompt the AI to draft a preface or an executive summary that encapsulates the main takeaways from each section, and perhaps an epilogue or concluding chapter that reflects on the journey as a whole. These integrative pieces ensure the final product doesn’t feel like a patchwork of disjointed parts, but rather a single, flowing narrative.

Using Divide and Integrate addresses a challenge we mentioned earlier: maintaining the big picture. By dividing, you gave focused attention to each major component of the project. By integrating, you stepped back to see the whole and polish the connections. This technique is powerful for very large tasks which would be impractical to handle in one go or even in one continuous thread. It’s how you would approach a big writing project or report in real life (you might assign different chapters to different team members, then have an editor combine them), and it works similarly well when orchestrating prompts for an AI.

**Bringing It All Together:**  
Across these techniques – Pipeline, Silo, Zoom In, and Divide and Integrate – the common thread is thinking ahead about the structure of the task. In practice, you might even combine these techniques. For example, in a multi-chapter report (Divide and Integrate), you might use a Pipeline approach within one of the chapters (first gather data, then analyze in writing), and use a Zoom In approach for another chapter (outline then detail). These patterns are not rigid silos themselves; they can overlap as needed. The goal is to give you a toolkit for approaching complex prompts systematically.

By applying decomposition, you turn a potentially overwhelming interaction with the AI into a series of manageable steps. You reduce the cognitive strain on the model and on yourself, ensure better coverage of all aspects of the problem, and often end up with a higher quality result. Just as importantly, you remain in the driver’s seat, guiding the AI through the task with clarity and purpose. Over time, using these techniques becomes second nature, and you’ll find that even very complicated projects can be handled smoothly by “delegating” to the AI one clear subtask at a time. This not only improves outcomes but also builds your confidence in tackling bigger and bigger problems with the help of LLMs.
